Summary
This paper investigates language-guided gradient inversion attacks (Geminio) in federated learning and asks how to defend against them. It reports a negative result that adversarially robust VLM encoders can strengthen Geminio’s targeting on medical imagery, and proposes a simple client-side defense—Norm-Bounded Federated Updates (NBFU), i.e., per-update L2 clipping plus Gaussian noise—arguing analytically that clipping caps the post-defense SNR irrespective of the attacker’s amplification, and showing empirically that NBFU substantially reduces attack F1 across two domains and multiple reconstruction algorithms.

Strengths
Technical novelty and innovation
The finding that adversarially robust VLMs strengthen language-guided GIAs on medical imagery is a timely, non-obvious, and useful negative result for practitioners who might otherwise rely on encoder robustness as a proxy for privacy.
The core defense leverages a standard but underappreciated insight in this threat model: norm-bounding at the client severs the attack’s dependence on loss-ratio amplification, connecting privacy protection to gradient-scale control.
Experimental rigor and validation
The defense is evaluated against multiple reconstruction algorithms (HF-GradInv, IG, GradInversion, DLG), two datasets, and several privacy budgets, with multi-seed reporting.
The paper includes useful ablations (privacy budget sweep, batch-size effects) and quantitative inspection of pre-clip gradient norms consistent with the defense rationale.
Clarity of presentation
The threat model and attack mechanism are clearly explained, and the empirical setup is easy to follow. The SNR argument, while informal, is intuitive and well-motivated in context.
The narrative distinguishes the semantic (VLM-side) signal from the gradient channel and articulates why encoder-side robustness does not transfer to gradient privacy.
Significance of contributions
Language-conditioned attacks are likely to grow stronger as VLMs scale; showing that simple clipping-based defenses remain invariant to that scaling is practically significant for FL deployments.
Releasing configs/code for attack and defense implementations can meaningfully reduce friction for follow-up work and reproducibility.
Weaknesses
Technical limitations or concerns
Differential privacy claims and calibration appear inconsistent with standard DP-SGD: per-update clipping without per-example clipping does not, by itself, bound per-record sensitivity; yet the noise formula used includes a 1/√n dependence, implicitly assuming per-example clipping. The (ε, δ) guarantee and neighboring relation (record-level vs client-level) are not rigorously specified.
The SNR analysis uses model dimension d ≈ 1.13×10^7 despite stating that only the classifier head is trainable. If frozen layers contribute zeros, adding noise in those coordinates inflates the noise norm and SNR bound but does not affect the information-carrying subspace. This risks overstating the theoretical and empirical protection.
The claim that “clipping, not noise, is the dominant factor” conflicts with scale invariance of gradient direction: in the absence of noise, clipping alone should not impair reconstruction when the attacker uses cosine objectives. The ε=50 result may still be dominated by noise if d includes frozen parameters.
Experimental gaps or methodological issues
No utility analysis is reported (e.g., task accuracy under NBFU across ε and C). Given the very aggressive clipping (16× and 527× shrinkage), the impact on model training/utility is critical to claim deployability.
The robust-VLMs-backfire section reports increased loss ratios but does not show corresponding end-to-end improvements (or degradations) in actual reconstruction quality metrics; the UAV result is mixed.
Batch sizes are small (n ≤ 32) and three GIAs could not be run at larger n; the defense margin shrinks with larger n, but the practical regime for many FL applications may use larger local batches or multiple local steps.
Clarity or presentation issues
The precise definition of the DP guarantee is ambiguous: is it example-level, batch-level, or client-level DP? What is the neighboring relation? How is composition across rounds handled?
The dimension d used in Eq. (4) is not clearly tied to the set of parameters over which the attacker computes the reconstruction loss.
Missing related work or comparisons
Prior work on clipping strategies and DP in FL (e.g., difference vs model clipping, convergence/utility trade-offs) should be discussed and contrasted (Zhang et al., 2106.13673). Methods that combine clipping with communication/DP gains (DPSFL, 2410.05733) and recent empirical assessments of DP vs GIAs (2510.23931) are relevant baselines or discussion points.
Non-DP defenses that manipulate gradient subspaces (e.g., SVD-based SVDefense, 2510.03319) or model-based stochastic defenses (CVB, 2309.04515) are not compared, though they target the same attack family.
Detailed Comments
Technical soundness evaluation
The main defense mechanism—client-side clipping plus Gaussian noise—is sound and aligns with established DP practice in spirit. However, the paper currently conflates per-update clipping with per-example clipping in its privacy calibration: using σ = C·sqrt(2 ln(1.25/δ)/(ε·n)) presumes bounded per-example contribution to the averaged gradient, which the method does not enforce. Without per-example clipping or additional assumptions (e.g., bounded per-example gradients), the stated record-level DP guarantee is not supported. If the intent is client-level DP per update, include a correct sensitivity and privacy accountant (and remove the 1/√n factor).
Using the full model dimension d when most parameters are frozen is analytically problematic: the attacker’s objective depends on gradients for trainable parameters; inflating d with zeros both weakens the SNR bound and can overstate the empirical efficacy of the noise. Please re-derive Eq. (4) using the dimensionality of the trainable parameter subspace and report ablations where noise is added only to those coordinates.
The assertion that clipping is the “load-bearing” component contradicts the invariance of cosine-based matching to scale. An ablation with σ=0 (pure clipping) is needed. If pure clipping indeed reduces F1, it likely reflects the use of Euclidean matching in some GIAs or additional nonlinearities; otherwise, the ε=50 results may be driven by noise magnitude when d is large.
Experimental evaluation assessment
The breadth across attacks, seeds, and datasets is appreciated. Still, the absence of task-utility results is a major gap: strong clipping and DP noise can degrade training; showing accuracy/learning curves is necessary to substantiate the “deployable” claim and to map the practical privacy–utility frontier.
The robust-VLMs result is interesting but would be more convincing with end-to-end attack outcomes (LPIPS/PSNR, retrieval F1) in addition to loss ratios, and with more domains/queries to probe generality.
The batch-size analysis is informative; given the observed sensitivity, an adaptive/tuned C (e.g., percentile of observed per-update norms) should be evaluated. Also consider multi-local-step settings, which are common in FL.
Comparison with related work (using the summaries provided)
2106.13673 (Zhang et al.) analyzes clipping choices in FL and provides guidance on threshold selection and bias/noise trade-offs. The present work should situate NBFU within that framework, clarify whether difference clipping could be used, and discuss expected utility impacts under heterogeneity.
2410.05733 (DPSFL) and 2506.03618 (GCFL) propose complementary approaches that reduce DP noise impact or correct privatized gradients post hoc. Positioning NBFU relative to these could improve the paper’s practical guidance.
2510.23931 provides empirical evidence that standard DP-SGD reduces reconstruction success while maintaining utility for large pretrained models; this corroborates the high-level message here but also underscores the need to report utility.
2309.04515, 2510.03319, 2208.06163 illustrate non-DP defenses and adaptive attacks; some of these should be included as baselines or discussed as complementary approaches, especially where DP budgets are constrained.
Discussion of broader impact and significance
The paper highlights an important and growing risk: language-guided, semantically targeted leakage. The result that clipping-based defenses are invariant to amplification argues for robust, future-proof mitigations as VLMs continue to improve. The practical tuning advice (pick C near the median per-update norm, then select ε) is useful, but must be matched with utility measurements and a corrected privacy accounting to be actionable.
Questions for Authors
What is the precise DP notion you claim for NBFU—record-level (per example), batch-level, or client-level? Please specify the neighboring relation and justify the noise calibration, especially the 1/√n dependence, given that you do not perform per-example clipping.
In Eq. (4), what exactly is d? Are you including frozen parameters? If yes, do you also add noise to those coordinates? Please report ablations where d and noise are restricted to trainable parameters only, and re-evaluate both the analytic SNR and empirical F1.
Can you provide an explicit σ=0 ablation (pure clipping) to test the claim that clipping, not noise, is the dominant factor—particularly for HF-GradInv with cosine matching?
What is the model utility under NBFU (accuracy vs rounds) across ε and different C settings for both domains? Does the model still converge with the aggressive clipping factors you report?
For the “robust VLMs backfire” result, can you include end-to-end reconstruction metrics (e.g., F1/LPIPS/PSNR) and more textual queries to support that higher loss ratios translate into stronger practical leakage?
How does NBFU compare to per-example DP-SGD (standard DP-FL) under matched ε, δ accounting and similar clipping thresholds?
How sensitive is the defense to the choice of C? Could you evaluate an adaptive clipping scheme (e.g., DPSFL-AC style adaptive thresholding) to stabilize protection as batch size varies?
Do your reconstruction back-ends use only the gradients of trainable layers? If so, is any added noise on frozen parameters irrelevant to the attacker’s objective?
Overall Assessment
This paper addresses an important and timely problem at the NLP–vision–privacy intersection: language-guided GIAs in federated learning. The empirical finding that adversarially robust VLMs can strengthen the attack on medical imagery is noteworthy, and the main defense—client-side norm bounding with Gaussian noise—performs well across multiple attack back-ends and domains. The analytical framing that clipping de-couples defense effectiveness from the attacker’s amplification provides a useful intuition. However, there are significant issues that need resolution before publication at a top-tier venue. Most critically, the DP guarantee and noise calibration are not rigorously justified given the stated procedure (per-update rather than per-example clipping), the SNR analysis appears to rely on an inflated dimensionality that may not reflect the attacker-relevant subspace, and the central claim that clipping (not noise) is decisive is not convincingly supported without a σ=0 ablation. The absence of training-utility results also weakens the case for deployability. With these concerns addressed—especially a corrected privacy accounting, clear dimensionality/noise treatment, utility–privacy curves, and a pure-clipping ablation—this work could make a valuable contribution. In its current form, I recommend rejection or a strong revise-and-resubmit.
