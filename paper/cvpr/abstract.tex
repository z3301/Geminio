\begin{abstract}
Federated learning lets hospitals and drone operators train shared models without pooling images, yet a malicious server can still rebuild private training images from the gradients clients send.
Geminio sharpens this threat: the server describes the images it wants in natural language, and a vision-language model rigs the shared model so that matching images dominate each client's gradient.
Adversarially robust CLIP encoders are increasingly used to harden vision-language models, so we ask how they change this attack.
We evaluate Geminio on chest X-rays and aerial drone imagery with six robust encoders, used both as the attacker's guide and as the backbone of the client model.
Robustness provides no protection.
As the attacker's guide, robust encoders yield markedly more faithful reconstructions of the targeted chest X-rays for every encoder tested; as the client backbone, they make the attack on drone imagery stronger and more selective in proportion to their robustness, so the malicious head and the robust backbone reinforce each other.
We also identify where Geminio breaks down: indirect and terse descriptions, and targets that resemble the rest of the batch, whereas misspelled, translated and negated queries do not weaken it.
Finally, adding calibrated Gaussian noise to each clipped client update reliably defeats inverting-gradients reconstruction in both domains and the language-guided attack on drone imagery, whereas clipping alone gives no protection.
\end{abstract}
