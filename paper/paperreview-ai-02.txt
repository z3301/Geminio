Summary
This paper studies defenses against Geminio, a recently introduced language-guided gradient inversion attack that uses a vision–language model (VLM) to amplify gradients from samples that match a natural-language query. The authors make two main claims: (1) adversarially robust VLM variants do not mitigate this threat and can even strengthen it on medical imagery, and (2) a simple client-side mechanism—per-update L2 clipping followed by Gaussian noise, which the authors term Norm-Bounded Federated Updates (NBFU)—substantially reduces reconstruction success across multiple attack algorithms. They provide an intuitive SNR-based argument explaining why clipping renders the defense invariant to the attacker’s amplification factor and present a σ=0 ablation to disentangle the respective roles of clipping (disrupting scale-sensitive priors) and noise (breaking scale-invariant gradient matching).

Strengths
Technical novelty and innovation
The mechanistic decomposition that shows clipping and noise contribute via distinct mechanisms to stopping different classes of GIAs (scale-sensitive priors vs. scale-invariant cosine matching) is insightful and practically useful.
The SNR-based argument that clipping removes dependence on the attacker’s loss-ratio amplification is simple but clarifying for this emerging, language-guided attack class.
Experimental rigor and validation
The study evaluates across several reconstruction algorithms (HF-GradInv, IG, GradInversion; DLG noted as too weak at the tested scale), two domains (medical and UAV), and multiple random seeds.
The privacy-utility exploration (even if limited) is candid and acknowledges the cost of strong noise.
Clarity of presentation
The paper clearly articulates the threat model and how language-guided targeting interacts with gradient inversion.
The σ=0 ablation tables are easy to interpret and match the stated hypotheses about scale sensitivity.
Significance of contributions
The observation that adversarially robust VLMs can strengthen language-guided attacks in medical imagery is important and actionable for practitioners.
Demonstrating that (appropriately tuned) DP-style clipping+noise can neutralize the Geminio family of attacks is a practically significant result for FL deployments.
Weaknesses
Technical limitations or concerns
The DP semantics and sensitivity calibration are unclear and potentially incorrect. With per-update clipping (not per-example), the L2 sensitivity between neighboring datasets that differ by a single record in a client batch is at most 2C, not C; the text uses C in the Gaussian mechanism, which underestimates the required noise scale. The paper also uses the term “client-level DP” while defining a neighboring relation that substitutes a single record, which is inconsistent with standard client-level DP (presence/absence of a client’s entire dataset).
There is an internal inconsistency regarding which parameters are trainable: Section 3.2 computes SNR with d ≈ 1.13e7 (full ResNet-18 requires_grad=True) while the experimental setup states only the classifier head is updated. This mismatch undermines the SNR calculation and privacy-utility conclusions and needs reconciliation.
The privacy-utility experiment appears to apply NBFU at every training step using a per-step ε without composing over steps/epochs; reported ε values are therefore not end-to-end budgets, limiting interpretability of the privacy-utility frontier.
Experimental gaps or methodological issues
The “robust VLMs backfire” result is evaluated via loss-ratio amplification rather than full end-to-end reconstruction quality across VLM variants; stronger evidence would include F1/LPIPS comparisons for reconstructions guided by each VLM.
Evaluation focuses on gradient-matching optimizers; more powerful recent attacks that leverage generative priors or denoising (e.g., GIFD, GI‑SMN, GUIDE) are not tested. These may exploit image priors to partially overcome noisy gradients; testing them is important for claims of broad robustness.
Batch-size sweep is limited to one attack due to memory; several FL settings use larger batch sizes, where sensitivity and clipping thresholds matter most.
Clarity or presentation issues
Several references are missing (“?” placeholders) and the DP discussion includes ambiguous terminology (“client-level in the substitute-one-record neighboring relation”).
Table/figure labeling occasionally uses “DP-SGD” for NBFU (which is per-update, not per-example), risking confusion.
Missing related work or comparisons
The paper does not discuss or compare against recent defense proposals tailored to GIAs (e.g., interpretability-guided TIP) or more sophisticated attackers that withstand some defenses (GIFD, GUIDE). Related findings (e.g., 2510.23931) that DP-SGD empirically reduces gradient leakage are aligned but could be integrated more directly.
Detailed Comments
Technical soundness evaluation
The SNR argument is reasonable as an intuition: with L2 clipping at C and isotropic Gaussian noise calibrated to C (or 2C; see below), the defended gradient’s norm is O(C) while noise norm is O(σ√d), making SNR independent of the attacker’s amplification R. This helps explain why progressively stronger language-guided targeting does not overcome the defense.
However, the DP sensitivity calibration is problematic. Per-update clipping alone does not ensure record-level sensitivity C; the worst-case L2 difference between two clipped updates from neighboring batches differing in one record can be up to 2C. If the Gaussian mechanism is calibrated to C rather than 2C, the formal ε, δ claims do not hold as stated. More fundamentally, replacing per-example clipping with per-update clipping yields a different (weaker) protection notion that must be defined precisely; the paper’s term “client-level DP per round in the substitute-one-record neighboring relation” is nonstandard and conflates levels of protection.
The inconsistency regarding trainable parameters (full model vs. classifier-only) directly affects both the SNR computation and the utility assessment. If only the head is trainable (≈148k parameters), the noise norm and hence SNR differ by an order of magnitude; the defense’s empirical effectiveness might still hold, but the analytical and privacy-utility claims should reflect the correct d.
Experimental evaluation assessment
Multi-seed experiments, cross-attack comparisons, and the σ=0 ablation strengthen the empirical narrative. The UAV results (F1 near 0 across ε for HF-GradInv) demonstrate a compelling defense regime consistent with the scale-invariant cosine-matching story.
The medical-domain residuals and the batch-size dependence are thoughtfully analyzed: image priors and reduced clipping aggressiveness plausibly explain the remaining leakage and the shrinking defense margin at larger batches.
The “robust VLMs backfire” finding is interesting but would benefit from full reconstruction metrics and ablations across robust variants (the figure reports only some variants; TeCoA is mentioned but not shown), to move beyond loss-ratio alone.
Utility analysis is valuable but currently not tied to an end-to-end privacy budget: applying a per-step ε without composition weakens the takeaway. Also, the use of classifier-head-only training for utility, versus full-model parameters in SNR, raises questions about transferability of the conclusions to realistic FL settings.
Comparison with related work (using the summaries provided)
The claim that calibrated DP-style mechanisms reduce gradient leakage aligns with recent empirical observations (e.g., 2510.23931), which found that standard DP-SGD (per-example clipping + Gaussian noise) degrades reconstructions at a utility cost, whereas non-DP regularizers fail to protect privacy. This paper’s contribution is to tailor that insight to the language-guided active-server setting and to dissect clipping vs. noise contributions.
Several recent stronger attacks (GIFD: 2604.00955; GI‑SMN: 2405.03516; GUIDE: 2510.17621) leverage generative priors and post-hoc denoising, and have shown robustness against some defenses and moderate Gaussian noise. While very strong noise often degrades all methods, evaluating NBFU against at least one such attack would substantiate the “generalizes across reconstruction algorithms” claim beyond the gradient-matching family.
The work is well positioned relative to Geminio (2411.14937), calling out that simple noise/pruning were insufficient at practical scales and that language-guided amplification raises new stakes; this paper’s invariance-to-amplification argument is a meaningful response.
Recent systematizations (2508.19819) emphasize realistic training-mode assumptions (e.g., BatchNorm behavior) and show that many GIAs succeed only under inference-mode. The present paper should clarify the training-mode configurations used (e.g., BN mode) to avoid optimistic attack/defense evaluations.
Discussion of broader impact and significance
The paper underlines that advances in multimodal understanding can inadvertently increase privacy risk for FL, which is timely and relevant for the EMNLP community that develops VLMs. The negative result on robust VLMs helps redirect defense efforts toward the gradient channel.
Practically, the defense–utility trade-off remains a major barrier: the strongest protection regimes that thwart attacks also risk destabilizing training if used continuously. The proposed “round-targeted defense” is plausible but requires reliable anomaly detection, which is not developed here.
Questions for Authors
Please clarify the DP guarantee precisely: what is the neighboring relation (record-level within a client’s batch? client-level presence/absence?), and what is the correct L2 sensitivity for your mechanism (C or 2C)? If it is 2C, how do your empirical conclusions change when σ is doubled?
Section 3.2 computes SNR with d ≈ 1.13e7 while Section 4 states only the classifier head is trainable. Which parameters are updated in the defense and in the attack experiments? Please reconcile this and, if only the head is trainable, recompute/clarify the SNR and any conclusions that depend on d.
In the privacy-utility experiment, is ε reported per step or composed over the full training run? If per step, what is the end-to-end ε after 10 epochs, and how does the observed “cliff at ε ≈ 5–10” translate to a holistic privacy budget?
For the “robust VLMs backfire” result, can you report end-to-end reconstruction metrics (F1/LPIPS/PSNR) per VLM variant, not just loss ratios? Also, did you test TeCoA and SimCLIP/FARE with multiple robustness budgets and on UAV?
What is the training-mode configuration for BatchNorm and dropout during both attack and defense experiments? Did you consider the training-mode vs. inference-mode issues raised by 2508.19819?
Have you evaluated NBFU against at least one recent generative/denoising-augmented attack (e.g., GIFD, GI‑SMN, GUIDE)? If not, can you comment on expected behavior and whether the σ=0 ablation insights carry over?
The proposed “round-targeted defense” depends on detecting suspicious rounds. What detection signals or criteria would you recommend, and how robust are they to adaptive attackers (e.g., Geminio-adaptive)?
Overall Assessment
This paper addresses an important and timely problem at the intersection of multimodal learning and privacy: language-guided, active-server gradient inversion in federated learning. The empirical finding that adversarially robust VLMs can strengthen such attacks (in medical imagery) is noteworthy, and the central defense result—that clipping+Gaussian noise neutralizes the attack across several reconstruction methods—adds practical value. The mechanistic σ=0 ablation and SNR reasoning are particularly clear and actionable.

However, there are significant issues that limit the paper’s acceptability in its current form. Most importantly, the DP semantics and sensitivity calibration appear incorrect or at least ambiguous: per-update clipping does not provide record-level sensitivity C, and the “client-level” terminology conflicts with the defined adjacency. This undermines the claimed formal guarantees. There is also an inconsistency regarding which parameters are trainable, which affects the SNR logic and the privacy-utility analysis. Finally, the empirical scope, while solid for gradient-matching GIAs, does not test stronger modern attacks that use generative priors or denoising, and the “robust VLMs backfire” result is supported only by loss-ratio, not by full reconstruction metrics.

I view this as a promising and useful direction with several strong insights, but the technical DP claims and experimental coverage require clarification and extension. With corrected DP analysis/calibration, consistent parameter settings, and stronger empirical validation (including generative/denoising attacks and full metrics for robust VLMs), this work would make a valuable contribution. As is, I recommend rejection with encouragement to resubmit after addressing these issues.
